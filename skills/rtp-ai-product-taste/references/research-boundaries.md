# Research and example boundaries

This reference preserves the origins and limits of the skill's additional lenses. The main workflow uses them as questions to test, not settled laws of product quality.

## Experience recall

The local research collection cites Joseph C. Nunes and Wendy Heimann, “Why the Best Immersive Experiences Succeed,” *Harvard Business Review*, July–August 2026. The primary page confirms the title, authors, and issue. The one-week recall probe in this skill is the library's adaptation from immersive experiences to AI products; the available material does not establish its validation as an AI product measure. [Primary article](https://hbr.org/2026/07/why-the-best-immersive-experiences-succeed).

Use the probe to discover what users noticed and what changed. A presentation-focused recollection can suggest a benefit worth investigating, a weak connection to the job, or a product whose intended value is the experience itself. Absence of recall does not establish absence of value. Compare recall with task outcomes and avoid leading the respondent before free recall.

## Audience acceptance and Lily

The local source is HBR Cold Call, “How Duolingo Aims to Diversify Beyond Language Learning,” April 2025, discussing HBS case 825-097. A co-author reports being more willing to practice with Lily than with a Spanish speaker because mistakes feel less embarrassing. This is personal testimony, not an experiment or an estimate of user preference.

The Novel Insights ledger first proposed a distinction between AI experiences with and without a non-AI alternative, then narrowed its explanation to displacement of the provider's previous offering. The testimony challenges the first explanation; it does not uniquely establish the second. Alternative explanations include convenience, social comfort, cost, access, and perceived quality. The claim that Duolingo never offered a tutor-booking product was not independently verified in this editorial pass and is not needed to use the diagnostic.

The ledger also compares favorable commentary on the product with workforce reactions to an AI-first memo. Different stakeholders, outcomes, and reporting contexts prevent treating that comparison as a controlled matched pair. Preserve both perspectives without attributing the difference to a single cause.

## Proven–Better–New and learning taste

The local July 2026 HBR IdeaCast notes attribute the discipline to Mark Pincus. The worked anecdote concerns two competing social games whose novel features received attention while onboarding was neglected. The speaker's retrospective attribution may inform a design hypothesis; it does not demonstrate which choice caused the outcomes or how often this pattern holds.

The older wording's “user-stupidity asset” describes the builder's loss of a beginner's perspective, not a defect in the user. The revised term makes the intended action clearer: observe newcomers and inspect what they need from the interface. The roughly one-quarter timing was not an established universal threshold.

The ledger's later qualification of pattern E matters here: studying, comparing, and interrogating other products can help develop taste. Direct production is useful but not the only possible learning route. Conversely, observation alone does not establish the ability to perform every task. Evaluate the capability actually needed.

## Portfolio and depth

The local July 2026 HBR Cold Call notes describe Caroline Elkins discussing the Atlantic/OpenAI relationship. Preserve the distinction between selecting a candidate and assessing a finished artifact; do not infer undisclosed commercial terms or a universal causal effect of AI from the discussion. A single artifact can be revised repeatedly, and a portfolio can contain highly correlated weak candidates.

A useful counter-test asks whether AI assistance improves a finished artifact's distinctiveness and correctness under a fair comparison. For a portfolio, test whether generating more candidates improves the selected result after accounting for selection quality and cost. Neither structure settles the answer in advance.

## Numerical illustrations and prompt framing

The previous skill used free, $20/month, and $500/month tiers; an 80% acceptance marker; five to ten corner cases; and hypothetical +8% and +15% acceptance lifts for two prompt framings. These remain historical teaching examples, not validated thresholds, forecasts, or release criteria. Record denominators, samples, uncertainty, and whether a reported lift is relative or in percentage points before interpreting any real result.

Anthropic's harness report describes visual convergence associated with “museum quality” wording. That is evidence that framing can shape output character in the reported setup, not that those words reliably produce superior work in other domains. The skill therefore asks for domain criteria, controlled comparison, and outcome evaluation. [Primary engineering report](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/harness-design-long-running-apps).
