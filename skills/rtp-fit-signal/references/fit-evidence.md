# Fit Signal — Evidence and Measurement Notes

Reviewed 13 September 2026. Full original skill and concept guide read. The revision keeps longitudinal analysis, activation, corrections, feedback, preference/switching, four verdicts, and connected-skill handoffs. It changes the inference rules so a useful proxy cannot silently become proof.

## What the original score can and cannot measure

The 1.0/0.6/0.2/0.0 fidelity weights have no validation supplied in this library. Their average summarizes categorized output use, not perceived trust, error probability, or market fit. The revised name is **fidelity-weighted reuse index**; preserve a version note if migrating an existing dashboard.

No evidence reviewed establishes that this index is immune to output variance, selection, lucky runs, habituation, or reduced checking. Standard retention and recommendation measures do not mathematically require identical experiences on every use. They and the reuse index need clear definitions and interpretation.

The earlier minor/major definitions left 30–50% edits unclassified and confused amount changed with consequence. The revision uses a task-specific rubric and separates editing reason from magnitude. Regenerating a creative alternative is not necessarily failure. An output can be used without being checked; record that distinction instead of assuming correctness.

Repeated outcomes from the same user are correlated. A cohort mean can change when dissatisfied users leave, while surviving users’ experience is unchanged. Track eligibility, missingness, attrition, exposure, product version, and task mix. A flat high curve may be healthy; rising perceived trust with unchanged poor quality may be dangerous.

## Primary-source checks

**Product-market-fit surveys.** [Rahul Vohra’s Superhuman account](https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/) was read through the survey, benchmarking, and segmentation sections. It attributes the disappointment question to Sean Ellis and describes the team’s own use of it. The reported 40% benchmark and smaller-sample observation are practitioner evidence, not universal minimums or guaranteed inference. The earlier attribution of AARRR to Vohra was removed; it was not needed to explain his actual segmentation contribution.

**Trust citation.** Publisher/index metadata identifies [Lee and See, Human Factors 46(1), 50–80, 2004](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1518/hfes.46.1.50_30392) as the authors of *Trust in Automation: Designing for Appropriate Reliance*. The publisher text and university PDF did not load in this pass, so this revision does not claim a full-paper review or borrow precise findings from it. The original Elaine May attribution and unverified “Constitutional AI as Trust Infrastructure” title are not retained as evidence.

**Satisfaction and outcomes.** [Rice University’s account of Anderson and Mittal’s research](https://business.rice.edu/wisdom/peer-reviewed-research/product-marketing-customer-satisfaction) was read, and the 2000 publication record checked. It supports considering nonlinear and asymmetric relationships among satisfaction, retention, and profit. It does not validate a universal fifth-category cliff, prove that four-star ratings are uninformative, or establish an AI-specific fit threshold. The original unspecified Gallup meta-analysis was not independently identified here; no numerical effect is carried from it.

**Founder-led sales.** Full text of Rubinstein and Onyemah’s *Startup Founders Need a New Sales Playbook*, HBR, **24 June 2026**, read from the user’s PDF. The authors report more than 250 interviews conducted June–December 2025 across more than 30 countries, with the article’s analysis based on the **first 100**. The companies had $500,000–$10 million ARR. This is not solely a pre-revenue sample, a randomized test, or evidence that every interested buyer lacks intent.

SPRINT supplies Speed, Problem, Results, Implementation, Niche, and Trust. The skill retains these as diagnostic questions. The “AI made interest cheap” interpretation comes from the corpus, not a measured effect in the article. Trigger events can be elicited, silence has multiple explanations, and credibility can transfer beyond the founder.

**Instinct versus idea.** Read the relevant portions of the Mark Pincus *Creating Products with Curiosity, Humility, and Play* interview from the July-shelved user PDF: the opening distinction, four-or-five-variant advice, and discussion of learning from other products and feedback. The entire 5,040-word transcript was not re-reviewed for this skill. The interview’s “95% right/75% wrong” rhetoric is not a validated success rate. The distinction is useful only if evidence can also challenge the underlying belief; it must not immunize a favored direction from failure.

## Earlier thresholds and examples

| Earlier rule | Revised treatment |
|---|---|
| Eight weeks and 100 weekly users required | Choose evidence depth and observation windows for task frequency, independent units, variation, and decision consequence |
| Trust/index >0.60, inflection by week 4–5 | Illustrative trajectory only; no universal boundary between fit and absence |
| Activation >60% confirms fit; <20% means kill | Define opportunity and event, validate later value, and consider competing explanations |
| Correction reduction of 50–60% required | Measure task-specific corrective work and quality; report percentage points versus relative reduction |
| Code >90% usable, creative work 60–70% | No transferred acceptance requirement; editing can be part of intended use |
| 90% user confidence means a smaller verification market; 70% proves trust | Unsupported inference. Appropriate confidence and checking depend on task and consequence |
| Low deletion, >20% competitor return, >5–10% paid, or 50 interactions prove switching cost | Behavioral clues requiring reasons, opportunity, and denominators |
| 20–30-person competitor trial with >70% return confirms fit | Potential exploratory study, not a general benchmark or authorization to enroll users |
| <5% stated churn at +20% price supports premium | Hypothesis to assess against actual demand, revenue, alternatives, and uncertainty |
| No correction-driven product change means resignation | Several explanations remain; trace the feedback decision and verified outcomes |
| Any “yes” to four kill questions overturns fit | Answers have different meanings; the original downtime question even used the opposite direction from the combined rule |
| “Absent” has no condition worth revisiting | Scope the negative verdict and identify what materially new evidence or proposition would warrant review |
| Twenty-to-thirty percent weekly swings are normal | Diagnose the change and measurement noise; do not normalize a consequential regression |

The four concept cases are now explicitly fictional teaching comparisons. No supplied source establishes the original historical market winner, privacy-pivot effect, or six-month retention improvement. Their practical questions remain, with causal claims removed and units clarified.

The arithmetic example of six as-is, two minor, one major, and one rejected output still yields **0.74** and a **20%** major/rejection share. A decline from 60% to 15% is **45 percentage points**, or **75% relative**, not a generic 50–60% target.

## Novel Insights cross-check

Re-read the curiosity-and-play cluster and its observation/acceptance cautions, including the distinction between useful conceptual arguments and measured interventions. Preserve the prompts to examine competing explanations, selection, commitment, and feedback. Do not turn “cheap signals” into “no information,” an observed action into proof of trust, or a founder’s favored instinct into an unfalsifiable premise.

This scorecard is a decision aid. It does not provide a validated scalar measure of PMF, eliminate measurement uncertainty, or supersede release, safety, financial, and user-authorization constraints.
