# Workforce observation and learning

Use these structures when a capability decision changes the work through which people develop judgment. `judgment-guard` owns the intervention design; Capability Tracking holds the dated observations and dependencies that inform it.

## Observe both delivery and development

Separate current expertise from how it formed. A cohort can complete more work with AI while learning more, learning less, or learning different skills. Compare assisted output, retained ability, unfamiliar-task transfer, and appropriate reliance. Match the measure to the competence the role needs; unaided performance is particularly relevant to recovery duties and retained judgment, but is not the only valuable outcome.

The [KPMG 2026 study](https://kpmg.com/us/en/articles/2026/ai-redefining-career-success-empower-your-talent-thrive.html) involved **523 U.S. early-career professionals with less than 18 months at the firm**, using a domain-specific agent in realistic work. They were not 523 organizations. Its apprentice, delegator, and amplifier profiles describe observed patterns; they are not permanent identities or randomized treatments. A person can develop both domain judgment and AI fluency.

The [CCL 70–20–10 guidance](https://www.ccl.org/articles/leading-effectively-articles/70-20-10-rule/) emphasizes challenging experiences, developmental relationships, and coursework or training. It does not establish that AI removes 90% of learning. Identify which experiences actually changed, who lost useful feedback, and what evidence shows a development gap. A historical example of reducing a team from 200 people to 50 does not establish that the same change would preserve another team's capacity or learning.

## Inspect the task bundle, not just job counts

The earlier skill drew a two-branch lens from [MIT Sloan's “5 things to consider when working with AI”](https://mitsloan.mit.edu/ideas-made-to-matter/5-things-to-consider-when-working-ai): removing relatively simple tasks can increase the expertise demanded by remaining work; removing specialized tasks can make some work accessible to more people. These are mechanisms to examine, not guaranteed wage directions. Demand, output expansion, task creation, bargaining, and other conditions also matter.

Record which parts of the role change and which competencies remain necessary. Do not infer that every team should become smaller and more senior, or that shorter-task exposure maps directly to every lower-income worker. Use observed task composition and access to retraining for the actual population.

## Check what happens to effort and the learning environment

The earlier skill connects Angela Duckworth's 2026 HBR conference remarks to two inputs: productive learning effort and the quality of the learning environment. Preserve the distinction without assuming that less effort always means less learning. Removing repetition or extraneous difficulty can help; removing essential practice can hurt. AI can supply feedback and examples, but their correctness and instructional fit matter.

Ask:

1. What necessary practice did the workflow remove, retain, or replace?
2. What useful feedback, examples, or attempts became possible, and are they reliable?
3. What evidence shows growth in retained ability or transfer, rather than only speed, busyness, or satisfaction?

Working near the edge of one's ability can be a useful observation. It is not sufficient evidence of learning, and every difficult step need not be reintroduced. The conference remarks supplied a design lens, not a measured AI effect size or a universal causal rule.

## Give mentoring a specific target

The earlier skill adapts HBR's July 2026 practitioner article “Why Mentoring Matters More in the AI Era.” Its seven suggested competencies are a starting menu: critical thinking, professional judgment, pattern recognition, proactive communication, stakeholder management, prioritization, and navigating ambiguity. Select the competencies the work requires and describe observable progress.

Retain its six practical moves:

1. Clarify expectations for mentor and learner.
2. Recognize mentoring in workload and incentives.
3. Give mentors useful support and methods.
4. Match support to the skill gap; the reporting line may or may not be the right match.
5. Discuss decision reasons, rejected alternatives, and the evidence that changed a view.
6. Add practice and feedback to ordinary work where practical, alongside scheduled sessions when useful.

A short mentoring agreement can name goals, competencies, cadence, and commitments from each party. Four fields are convenient, not a scientifically optimal length. Hands-on learning may still exist, and learning platforms can provide practice and feedback; inspect the actual gap rather than assuming all foundational learning has disappeared.

## Use a four-step learning loop when it fits

The earlier skill adapts Duncan and Anderson's June 2026 HBR practitioner approach, “Help Employees Get Better—Not Just Faster—with AI.” It is a proposed practice, not an established guarantee of improved judgment.

1. **Form a preliminary view.** Define the task, audience, useful outcome, and what you expect to find. If you cannot yet assess the answer, get a rubric, expert support, or a suitable learning exercise rather than pretending to be the final reviewer.
2. **Compare, challenge, and extend.** Consider alternative approaches where they would reveal meaningful tradeoffs. Three variants are an option; a single well-examined answer can also support learning.
3. **Examine the gaps.** Where did the output differ from your view? Check which claims survive evidence and why. Include cases where the model was right.
4. **Keep a concise decision record.** Capture the conclusion, key evidence, uncertainty, and any warranted override. Ask for a useful rationale and observable decision basis, not hidden internal reasoning.

When helpful, end with one tested observation about where this tool works well or needs support. Sample these observations over time. Assess the quality of acceptance, rejection, and escalation, including missed errors. More overrides are not automatically better; fewer are not automatically evidence of disengagement.

The original connections to Dreyfus, Polanyi, and the jagged technological frontier support attention to expertise, tacit knowledge, and uneven task performance. They do not establish that all apprenticeship has vanished or that prompting skills and professional judgment cannot develop together.
