# Strategy Canvas — Evidence and Planning Notes

Editorial review: 13 September 2026. These notes distinguish cited perspectives, verified figures, illustrative examples, and this library's own reasoning.

## Nine questions for a strategy discussion

These questions are the library's operational prompts around purpose, urgency, and integration. They are not verbatim questions from David De Cremer's article and are not a mandatory questionnaire for every use.

**Purpose**
1. If this succeeds, which important capability or customer outcome becomes possible over the relevant horizon?
2. How does the objective serve organizational purpose, and where might the metric be an incomplete proxy?
3. Can someone outside the team explain why the work matters?

**Urgency**
4. Would a different decision deadline change the preferred use case, and why?
5. Which deadlines are real constraints, and which are inherited assumptions?
6. What valuable alternative might be overlooked because it is harder to measure now?

**Integration**
7. Who owns the connection between this initiative and the strategy?
8. Can engineering, design, finance, and affected users understand the same underlying rationale, even when each needs different detail?
9. Does the roadmap support the intended outcome, whether that is a new value category, a necessary operational improvement, or both?

De Cremer's [July 2026 HBR article](https://hbr.org/2026/07/when-developing-an-ai-strategy-beware-the-urgency-trap) discusses how urgency can distort AI initiative selection. The local research note provides the longer source discussion. Do not turn this diagnosis into a claim that most AI failures have one cause or that cost and speed improvements cannot serve a meaningful purpose.

## Correct the Upwork figures before reuse

[Upwork's July 9, 2025 summary](https://www.upwork.com/blog/when-ai-becomes-a-teammate-what-upworks-research-reveals-about-the-relational-shifts-ai-is-driving-at-work) describes a survey of 2,500 workers, including executives, employees, and freelancers. It says 77% of executives observed productivity gains, reports productivity improvements of 40% for many workers, and says 88% of the most productive full-time employee group reported burnout. That group was twice as likely to consider quitting as peers.

Those are different measures and populations. The earlier skill's wording—output increased 77%, burnout increased 88%, in a 2026 population—was unsupported. The source does not establish the claimed quitting comparison specifically between workers who do and do not see strategic alignment. These survey associations do not show that AI caused burnout or that a clearer canvas will prevent it.

The 77% figure in [Upwork's 2024 study](https://www.upwork.com/research/ai-enhanced-work-models) instead concerns employees reporting added workload and lower productivity. Do not merge the two years. Also avoid confusing the 2025 summary's burnout prevalence with the wording in a later Upwork article that describes an 88% relative likelihood increase; they are different statistical claims. Use the original report and methods for consequential quantitative reuse.

## Constraint-first design

Winter and Govindarajan's [August 2026 HBR IdeaCast](https://hbr.org/podcast/2026/08/the-innovation-strategy-most-companies-miss) presents constraint-first innovation and cross-market adaptation. It is a practitioner conversation with selected successful cases, not a comparative effect-size study. The transfer to tired AI reviewers, incomplete context, vague prompts, and constrained devices is this library's application.

Test whether the targeted conditions share enough core value to support one design. Consider affordability, portability, infrastructure, performance, maintainability, and stakeholder incentives. Do not assume adaptations are always small or that exposure is the only intervention. Technical barriers and conflicting requirements can be real. Compare the cost and performance of constrained-first and well-resourced-first approaches when both are plausible.

## Moats and strategic centers

The previous text ranked technology-centered strategy below customer- and ecosystem-centered strategies using a McGrath management-tip discussion and Novel Insights K/N. That ranking was the library's inference, not a demonstrated result from the cited typology.

A widely rentable capability may erode differentiation, but lower technical costs can also improve margin, expand demand, or reward differentiated model developers. Customer relationships and ecosystems can themselves be copied, weaken, or fail to capture value. Evaluate the organization's specific advantage and strategic control rather than applying a universal ranking. Proprietary data and regulatory access also require a mechanism and appropriate rights; their labels do not establish defensibility.

Novel Insights L/Q use modification of a shared artifact as a signal of participation. It can be informative, but unchanged acceptance is not proof of compliance and disagreement is not a mandatory success condition. Apply the clearer participation guidance in `purpose-dialogue`.

## Planning numbers are examples

The original 30-second explanation, one-page limit, three objectives, two-to-four pillars, three conditional bets, and 90-day review are useful design prompts. Scale them to the task. Do not add filler, hide necessary evidence, or manufacture conflict to satisfy them.

The original strategy horizon bands and 15% performance/40% price/four-week evidence reset examples are not empirical rules. Define relative versus percentage-point changes, total cost versus token price, relevant test coverage, and the actual investment decision. A smaller improvement may matter; a larger one may not.

The legal-workflow example's 85% task-success threshold and fewer than two errors per ten steps have different units. Neither is a universal authorization for autonomous operation. The primary skill replaces the ambiguous release instruction with explicit task, consequence, and operating requirements.
