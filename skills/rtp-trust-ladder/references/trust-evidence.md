# Trust Ladder: evidence and limitations

Reviewed 13 September 2026. The original skill mixed useful design lenses with unsupported numerical gates. Fixed rejection targets, 63% explanation effects, two- to threefold trust-loss speed, and a numerical enterprise trust equation lack a traceable supporting study in this material. Preserve the mechanisms as questions; do not present those values as benchmarks.

## Five agent-adoption techniques

The full user-provided HBR article **To Adopt AI at Scale, Employees Need to Trust Agents**, Thomas McKinlay, Stefano Puntoni, and Serkan Saka, **9 September 2026**, was read. It synthesizes studies and practitioner accounts. Its 59% reported agentic use and 9% autonomous-workflow figures use different constructs and do not identify a causal effect of trust or show that every deployment should become autonomous.

The article reports up to 14.6% higher perceived transparency, up to 7.2% better collaboration, a 54% acceptance increase, and a 13.8% privacy-risk difference. These are contextual research results, not production targets. An effect on acceptance or perceived risk does not establish better decisions.

Targeted passages in the [April 2026 Wharton Blueprint](https://ai.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Wharton-Blueprint-for-AI-Agent-Adoption.pdf) clarify two boundaries. Page 25 describes the 54.2% result as conditional on long-term or virtue choices; pleasure-oriented choices can respond differently, citing Liu et al., *Psychology & Marketing* (2025). Page 38 places the privacy result in retail/service contexts, citing Sohn et al., *Journal of Retailing* (2025), and notes reversal when actual decision power is salient. These pages were checked; the underlying studies and the whole 47-page blueprint were not fully reviewed here. Do not misrepresent authority, invent precision, or claim continuous learning merely because a presentation technique increased trust.

## Disclosure, review competence, and sepsis

The Novel Insights ledger correctly questions attribution of a 41% mortality reduction to Cleveland Clinic. The [23 September 2025 Cleveland Clinic release](https://newsroom.clevelandclinic.org/2025/09/23/cleveland-clinic-announces-the-expanded-rollout-of-bayesian-healths-ai-platform-for-sepsis-detection) does not report that figure for its deployment. It describes a pilot and broader rollout, discusses an earlier study at other hospitals, and discloses an investment in Bayesian Health.

The interview’s roughly 10% clinician-only catch detail lacks a disclosed population and denominator in the material reviewed. It is a reported observation, not an independently verified detection rate or evidence that explanation cannot improve competence. Retain the clinical lesson as a question: does the combined workflow catch errors that either participant would miss? Do not infer a universal causal model from the story.

Explanations can help people verify evidence, learn, or notice inconsistencies; they can also persuade without improving correctness. Evaluate both. Do not reduce all safety effects to skills that existed before the explanation, and do not treat increased uptake as equivalent to safety.

## Hiring suspicion

Targeted primary passages in the user’s **AI Has Broken Hiring. Here’s How to Fix It.**, Shraddha Sunil and Mudit Saraf, HBR, **8 June 2026**, were read. The authors report 6,380 recorded first-round screens from eight US technology companies and interviews with 120 recruiting leaders across 87 companies. Automated checks used latency, vocabulary or fluency shifts, and gaze patterns; two or more signals led to manual review. Nearly 60% in one new-graduate software subgroup were flagged as suspicious, not proven to have cheated. The authors co-founded interview-screening company MeetGinger.

This is not a validated fraud detector or a recommendation to deploy those signals. Disability, language, assistive technology, environment, and connection quality can affect them. Human review of the same ambiguous evidence is not automatically independent confirmation. Give people a fair opportunity to clarify relevant facts and evaluate the actual job requirements. Broader automatic protective actions can be justified in other contexts, but an uncertain suspicion about a person is not itself proof for an adverse verdict.

## The 604-person knowledge-hiding survey: citation recovered

The full user-provided **Why Employees Aren’t Transparent About Their AI Usage**, **Eric Anicich and Jeslyn Brouwers**, HBR, **10 June 2026**, was read. This resolves the original skill’s missing title, authors, and date.

The authors surveyed 604 US employees using AI daily or multiple times a day and also interviewed professionals. They report 30.3% withholding AI-related methods and nearly four in five recognizing benefits from sharing. Lowest- versus highest-trust quartiles reported 47% versus 14% hiding, approximately 3.36 times, not a literal fourfold rate. Psychological-safety quartiles were 45% versus 17%. Trust was **one of** the strongest predictors, measured through seven items and retained after several controls.

The article reports attenuation after accounting for psychological safety and a stronger trust/hiding association with sanctioned tools. These observations are compatible with the proposed mechanism but do not establish causal mediation or the effect of installing tools. The authors offer interpretations involving both shareable infrastructure and fear of extraction. Hiding knowledge and hiding AI use are different outcomes; do not compare the 30.3% directly with KPMG’s concealed-use percentage as if the denominators and questions matched.

Practical responses include clear boundaries, a fair allocation of benefits, credit, lightweight sharing, and limiting the continuing support burden on contributors. Do not turn the findings into a rule that all approved tools log everything or that all low-trust rollouts cause concealment.

## Choice during interruptions: a material routing correction

The full user-provided **Research: When Consumers Have More Control Over Ads, They Respond Better**, **Siddharth Bhattacharya, Debashish Ghose, and Gordon Burtch**, HBR, **3 June 2026**, was read. It summarizes a working paper with **three studies and over 1,300 participants**, not a single study. Study 1 used imagined scenarios; studies 2 and 3 separately examined timing and content choice during a four-minute video, including eye tracking. The authors report 9–15% greater visual attention and 8–17% lower annoyance versus no-choice controls. Their comparison found no statistically distinguishable effect-size difference; this is not universal proof of equivalence.

The old skill reversed the commitment rule. The article recommends timing choice for committed viewers who are unlikely to defer and leave; low-commitment viewers are more prone to “delay and stray.” Predictable sessions can support timing choice. Content choice needs meaningful inventory and cognitive capacity; unfamiliar options can favor timing when feasible. The primary includes trade-offs among advertiser, platform, and viewer interests. Their AI-approval translation must consider genuine consent, safety, completion, and recourse rather than optimizing captive attention. [Author-institution summary](https://insights.bu.edu/research-when-consumers-have-more-control-over-ads-they-respond-better/).
