# Alignment Check — Research and limits

This reference separates evidence used in the original skill from the diagnostic guidance drawn from it. A case can motivate a question without establishing a universal rule. Local-source figures below are preserved for traceability; they were not all independently reverified in this editorial pass.

## The “93% of failures” claim

The original skill repeatedly said that 93% of AI failures are organizational rather than technical. The primary [2026 AI & Data Leadership Executive Benchmark Survey](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/62adf3ca029a6808a6c5be30/t/6942c3cb535da44088c2dbff/1765983179572/2026%2BAI%2B%26%2BData%2BLeadership%2BExecutive%2BBenchmark%2BSurvey%2BFinal.pdf), authored by Randy Bean, reports **93.2% of respondents** identifying cultural challenges and change management as their greatest impediment to adoption, with 6.8% selecting technological challenges. Its participants were invited senior AI/data leaders from major organizations. This is a reported assessment of obstacles, not a causal audit of failed projects or a representative failure rate for all AI work.

The concept guide also attributed a 90% pilot-failure rate to Gartner, a broad organizational-failure assertion to McKinsey, and an 8% large-scale-success rate to BCG, without exact primary citations, populations, or definitions. These remain unverified research leads and should not be used as evidence for the five-link model. Do not combine unlike survey measures into a single failure statistic.

## Independent expectations and shared definitions

**Dhar, Ellmer, and Jameson, “The False Alignment Trap,” HBR, July–August 2026 issue.** The [publisher page](https://hbr.org/2026/07/the-false-alignment-trap) confirms the issue; the local dossier supplies the detailed case. At an unnamed North American energy distributor, 10 of 13 executives reported clarity and 8 of 13 perceived team alignment, yet independently written accounts of the transformation differed materially. This supports using a concrete independent comparison; it cannot establish that all verbal checks fail or that all claimed agreement should be presumed false.

The authors' use of “alignment” for shallow assent and “agreement” for explicit commitments is their conceptual distinction, not a universal definition. The local dossier names false-consensus effects, expected discomfort of disagreement, and schedule pressure as possible mechanisms. Test what is happening in the current group instead of assigning all three automatically.

The AI/IT conflict material describes **three anonymized advisory cases**, with no quantified outcomes, concerning definitions of data, competence, and foundation. Shared classification is a useful diagnostic intervention. Continued disagreement under shared terms is evidence to examine substantive constraints. A common-parent settlement needs a shared decision record when different private accounts could impede execution; an org chart alone cannot establish whether this happened.

The goal-and-role instrument comes from **“How the Best Leaders Shape Conversations,” HBR, August 2026** in the local library. Use its practical questions without claiming that declaring authority guarantees an effective decision.

## Urgency, readiness, and trust

**Urgency trap:** the earlier skill cites a July 2026 HBR piece using Upwork/Workplace Intelligence survey reporting: output “up as much as 77%,” burnout “up 88%,” and regular AI users twice as likely to consider quitting when unable to connect work with strategy. The extract does not sufficiently establish the exact denominators, whether changes are relative, or comparable measurement for every figure. Keep them as source leads until the underlying questions and sample are checked. They do not prove that output gains caused burnout or describe an expected rate in a new organization.

**Readiness triad:** appetite, capacity, and skillset are attributed to Morris, HBR, August 2026. The local note describes a consultant's framework with several composite illustrations and no outcome validation. Its categories are useful. They do not establish a universal no-partial-credit rule or prove that adding people cannot create capacity.

**Accountable-versus-able matrix:** the earlier attribution is “3 Questions to Pressure-Test Your Priorities,” HBR, July 2026, with a ten-person workshop. The cross-corpus C/R synthesis adds a clearer interpretation of all four cells. This is a diagnostic lens, not a validated scale. In particular, unaccountable power can be risky too, and an observer lacking both responsibility and authority may be entirely appropriate.

**Anicich and Brouwers, “Why Employees Aren't Transparent About Their AI Usage,” 10 June 2026.** The [publisher page](https://hbr.org/2026/06/why-employees-arent-transparent-about-their-ai-usage) confirms authors and date. The supplied local material reports a survey of **604 daily AI-using US employees**, with **47%** reporting hiding in the lowest-trust quartile versus **14%** in the highest. It also reports a moderation involving sanctioned tools. These self-reported, cross-sectional associations do not establish causation, extend automatically to non-daily users or other countries, or justify removing useful monitoring. Check how local trust, incentives, and actual records are experienced.

## Ownership and preparedness

**“Data Transformation Is the CEO's Business,” MIT Sloan Management Review, 21 May 2026:** the local source reports Caterpillar's **14 data domains**, roughly **a dozen VP owners**, and monthly quality reports. This is company-disclosed case evidence. The proposed lesson is ownership transfer with visibility and practical authority when reusable components replace older system boundaries; the exact role level and cadence need not transfer.

**UNHCR leadership interview, “Reinventing an Organization to Do More with Less,” HBR IdeaCast, June 2026:** the supplied account describes eight years of prior change, an eighteen-month funding contraction, roughly 40% budget growth over a decade while the served population roughly doubled, and measures including decentralized decisions, shared services with a partner, cash-based assistance, and an innovation office/accelerator. These are reported organizational figures and narrative, not a controlled comparison. The source also describes limits to doing more with less. Prior reform may help resilience; it does not establish that resilience cannot be built during a crisis, or that every unforced reform is valuable.

**Transformation-readiness argument:** the local AI/mindset piece proposes radical targets, evaluation before scale, and unified data. Treat these as proposals to assess, not universal prerequisites. Its reported GDPval “about 80%, up from 50% six months earlier” lacks a sufficiently specific model, metric, version, and date in the skill's citation. It is not used as a verified current benchmark or a reason to abandon expertise. Any later use needs the primary benchmark definition and exact comparison.

## Boards, senior roles, and sponsor continuity

The original skill cites **“How C-Suite and Board Roles Are Being Reshaped Around AI,” HBR, June 2026**, and an executive-search firm's analysis of **5,000+ job descriptions**. It reports changing CFO emphasis toward analytics, AI, cloud, and security, and CHRO emphasis toward workforce analytics, human-AI collaboration, and relevant governance. Check the actual role before applying those trends. A job-ad analysis is not evidence of performance, and the firm's commercial interest should remain visible.

Its board model moves from peripheral AI to productivity use, AI-ready governance, agent contributions, and full delegation, with an additional “unknown unknowns” apex mentioned in the local material. The final endpoint is described as a warning. There is no validated sequence, measured ceiling on strategic understanding, or requirement to advance through the stages. The related claim that AI commoditizes all hard expertise is too broad to use as a hiring rule.

The primary [Spencer Stuart S&P 500 C-Suite Snapshot 2025](https://www.spencerstuart.com/-/media/2025/12/sp-500-snapshot/sp-500-csuite-snapshot-2025-profiles-in-functional-leadership.pdf) reports **59%** internal appointments overall and **80%** for COOs. It gives sitting CEO average tenure as **7.6 years**, not the original skill's 7.8. These are descriptive study figures, not audited individual predictions. The cited snapshot does not establish the original 57% of external hires with identical prior titles, roughly 75% for CFOs, or a claimed 16-year internal tenure; those figures need their own matching primary basis before reuse.

The original also reports at least one in five CFO/CHRO/CMO departures during a CEO's first year, and four-year departures of more than a third of legal chiefs, 42% of CTOs, and 39% of supply-chain chiefs, with most teams replaced within four years. These CEO-relative transition claims are not established by the cited cross-sectional snapshot in this check. Preserve them as unverified leads, not a sponsorship half-life calculation. Distinguish an incumbent's tenure, a new appointment rate, and turnover following a new CEO.

The personality-diversity discussion cites **“How Strong Teams Leverage Different Personality Types,” HBR, May 2026**, as argument-level material. Use the question of whether differences were examined, not the unsupported test that someone must change their mind in three meetings for alignment to be real.

## NOVEL INSIGHTS used in this revision

The revised reader guide and the C/R matrix, later decision-path refinements, Q review conditions, D adoption qualifications, and B capacity findings were checked against their actual entries. They support practical authority, shared decision records, honest disagreement, and real resources. The later School of Rock synthesis suggests that co-authorship can address some resistance to a standard; it remains a contextual hypothesis, not the only mechanism of adoption.

Several old formulations were bounded rather than repeated: formal reporting lines do not capture every influence path; artifacts are not proof of commitment; lack of direct override does not automatically imply dishonest explanations; and historical success does not guarantee a new deployment's readiness. The original dated ledger entries remain available for comparison.
