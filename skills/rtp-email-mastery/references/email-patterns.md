# Email voice references and writing checks

Companion revision 1.0.1, 13 Sep 2026. These examples explain choices; do not paste personal facts into a message without checking them.

## Preserve the voice without inventing a scene

Ravi's earlier guidance asks for honesty, warmth, thoughtful revision, familiar words, and respect for time. "Write from the heart" means communicate sincere intent. It does not mean skip factual reasoning or begin every email with an emotional monologue. "Clever words" should make a thought clearer, not draw attention away from it.

Useful principles are specificity, honesty, generosity, patience, and recognition of the other person's real effort. They can support trust, but they are not a validated equation or guarantee of response. Vulnerability can be appropriate when sincere and relevant; it is not a tactic to manufacture reciprocity.

Earlier opening examples included "It's late," "I just got home after 10+ hours of firefighting," "I saw your name tonight," and "What you said changed how I see this." Use them only when they accurately describe Ravi's situation. A simple "Thank you for registering" is complete and respectful without an invented backstory.

Earlier subject examples included "This email isn't automated" and "You're not lead #42." Do not imply hand-authorship, manual sending, or a unique one-to-one relationship unless true. AI help does not require a generic disclosure in every email, but follow applicable expectations and answer honestly if authorship matters. The Novel Insights observation about personal attention is a prompt to consider context, not a rule to conceal assistance or prohibit it in all personal notes.

## The 24 writing-pattern checks

These are editorial diagnostics, adapted from the older skill's Humanizer guidance. They are not an AI detector, and a legitimate use or exact quotation should not be changed mechanically.

| Pattern | Revision question |
|---|---|
| 1. Inflated significance | Can the specific fact replace an unsupported claim of importance? |
| 2. Notability lists | Does each credential or media mention help this recipient assess the message? |
| 3. Empty participial analysis | Does a phrase such as "highlighting" explain a real consequence or merely decorate the sentence? |
| 4. Promotional language | Can the actual feature, event, or outcome replace a superlative? |
| 5. Vague attribution | Who said or found this, and does attribution matter to the claim? |
| 6. Formulaic adversity | Can a real constraint and response replace "despite challenges"? |
| 7. Inflated vocabulary | Would a familiar, precise word make the sentence easier to read? |
| 8. Avoiding simple verbs | Would "is," "has," or a specific action express the thought more directly? |
| 9. Staged contrasts | Is the contrast necessary to explain a distinction, or does it add drama without meaning? |
| 10. Forced groups of three | Does the content naturally need this many items? |
| 11. Synonym cycling | Can one clear term keep the subject consistent? |
| 12. False ranges | Do the endpoints belong to a meaningful sequence or spectrum? |
| 13. Em-dash overuse | Can ordinary sentence structure improve the flow? Preserve exact quotations and syntax. |
| 14. Excessive bold | Is emphasis helping the recipient find important information? |
| 15. Inline-header lists | Would prose read better, or does a labeled list make parallel actions easier to scan? |
| 16. Title-case headings | Use sentence case where headings are needed; preserve proper names. |
| 17. Decorative emoji | Does the relationship and requested tone support it? Default to plain professional text. |
| 18. Quote styling | Follow the shared preference for straight quotes while preserving source text and functional syntax. |
| 19. Chatbot residue | Remove assistant-facing offers, greetings, or commentary accidentally included in the email. |
| 20. Generic cutoff disclaimers | Verify changing facts and state a specific access or evidence limit when it matters. |
| 21. Flattery | Replace reflexive praise with a sincere, specific acknowledgment. |
| 22. Filler | Remove phrases that do not change meaning, such as unnecessary "in order to." |
| 23. Stacked hedges | State one accurate qualification rather than several vague ones. |
| 24. Generic positive ending | End with the actual sentiment, next step, or information the occasion needs. |

Review the whole message after sentence edits. Keep a natural rhythm and appropriate structure. Do not add opinions, first-person claims, or deliberate messiness just to make the result seem human.

## Fast scenario map

| Situation | Starting pattern |
|---|---|
| Registration thanks | Specific acknowledgment, necessary next step, warmth |
| Follow-up with a professor | Actual discussion, agreements versus open questions, owners and dates |
| Stakeholder approval | Decision requested, consequence, recommendation, constraints |
| Admiration outreach | Specific work, relevance, brief introduction, manageable request |
| Reconnection | Real shared context and present reason for writing |
| Inactive learner | Neutral check-in and a small, practical way to return |
| Institution proposal | Relevant connection, verified credibility, concrete educational proposal |
| Milestone supporter thanks | Their actual contribution and its meaning |

An email can include a real deadline, a fair price, an apology, a clear boundary, or several related actions. Clarity and honesty determine whether these belong; no category requires exaggerated warmth or a fixed number of sentences.
