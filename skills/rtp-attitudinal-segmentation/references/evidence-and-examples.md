# Evidence and numerical-example boundaries

## Origin of the attitudinal lens

Hilary Gridley's contribution to Lenny's 2024 “Counterintuitive advice for building AI products” distinguishes AI enthusiasts and skeptics, recommends including both in early testing, and emphasizes engagement over time. It is practitioner evidence that attitude can matter. It does not establish a validated three-class survey, classification accuracy, or universal retention benefit. [Original discussion](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/counterintuitive-advice-for-building).

This library adds Neutral, Mixed, and Unknown categories and applies the lens to product decisions. The broader adoption categories draw inspiration from technology-adoption work associated with Geoffrey Moore; they should not be presented as a psychometric scale or a precise reproduction of a single published taxonomy. Craft orientation is a separate hypothesis about a user's relationship to the work.

The original Sai/Riya vignette gave their ages as 58 and 25 and experience as roughly forty and two years. Those details made the contrast concrete but do not explain the attitude. Both experienced enthusiasts and technically sophisticated skeptics are possible. The revised vignette retains the craft, sensor, review, and feedback issues while avoiding an age-based default.

## Needs and workplace responses

The local collection cites Hermann, Puntoni, and Morewedge's “Why Gen AI Feels So Threatening to Workers,” HBR, **March–April 2026**, and MIT SMR's “The Human Side of AI Adoption: Lessons From the Field.” Treat the needs framework as synthesis and the workplace examples as contextual evidence, not a measured rule that every resister has the same cause. [HBR primary article](https://hbr.org/2026/03/why-gen-ai-feels-so-threatening-to-workers).

The earlier reference to **AWARE** points to a response sequence: acknowledge concerns; watch for coping responses; align support with needs; redesign workflows for human–AI collaboration; and empower people through transparency and inclusion. Use it proportionately. Observed quiet use, disengagement, or unread output does not by itself establish a psychological cause or justify calling the person maladaptive.

The Novel Insights ledger adds useful questions about change costs, who retains the gains, and whether the affected person can shape the work. The School of Rock account illustrates invited co-authorship, without a comparison group proving its causal effect. Do not make co-authorship a universal prerequisite or infer that benefit framing cannot work without it.

## Survey figures: intent, use, and disclosure are different

BCG's **AI at Work 2025** reports that 54% of surveyed respondents would use AI tools even without company authorization, with n=10,635 for the study. This is reported willingness, not an observed rate of unauthorized use. The study is a survey and does not establish the cause of any individual's behavior. [Primary BCG report](https://web-assets.bcg.com/fd/0d/bcc5dfae4cbaa08c718b95b16cf5/ai-at-work-2025-slideshow-june-2025-edit-02.pdf).

Ivanti's **2025 Technology at Work** reports that 32% of respondents using gen AI at work keep it secret from their employer. The denominator is AI users at work, not all employees. Reported reasons include a private advantage, concern about job loss, and worry about how ability will be judged. The primary vendor report supports that attribution; it does not prove that hidden users all trust AI, that the organization is solely at fault, or that their use is unauthorized. [Primary Ivanti report](https://www.ivanti.com/resources/research-reports/tech-at-work).

Do not combine the BCG and Ivanti percentages as if they describe the same sample or outcome. Stated intent, actual use, declaration, authorization, and realized value require separate measures. Treat anonymous surveys and operational logs according to their respective limits.

## Historical values retained as illustrations

The previous skill used the following values to make its examples concrete. They were not supported operating standards and must not be used as default forecasts or gates:

| Earlier example | Correct interpretation |
|---|---|
| Approximately 70% behavioral classification accuracy | A hypothetical classifier result; validate labels and performance locally. |
| Approximately 3× retention from differentiated defaults | An illustrative effect, not an observed promise. Define cohort and comparison before making a retention claim. |
| First skeptical tester worth 5× another enthusiast | A rhetorical recruitment example, not a measured relative value of people. |
| 5+ features versus 0–1 explored in week one | Possible behavioral differences to examine; not reliable identity labels. |
| Familiarity 4–5/5, 2–3/5, or 1–2/5 | Familiarity does not determine attitude or action preferences. |
| First value in <10 minutes versus 2–4 weeks | Timing depends on task frequency, setup, quality, access, and support. |
| 90-second, four-minute, or twelve-minute onboarding | Design prototypes, not segment requirements. Twelve minutes split over three sessions is one possible approach. |
| Seven-, fourteen-, or thirty-day trials | Business and task choices to test, not fixed psychology. |
| Churn in week one or two; conversion by week three; loss by week four | Unsupported timing predictions. Observe actual behavior and alternatives. |
| Adjacent use cases introduced in weeks six to eight | A possible rollout plan, not a universal expansion schedule. |
| Fewer than 20 users, or wait until 100 users | Conflicting old cutoffs. Qualitative work can start before launch; quantitative sample needs follow the question and precision required. |
| 95% similarity, >10% quarterly drift, or ≥30% month-two retention lift | Candidate metrics requiring definitions and justification, not general decision thresholds. |
| 25 enthusiasts and five skeptics in research | A sample whose usefulness depends on the question; report unequal precision and coverage. |

The old L1–L4 segment defaults described draft, approval, and automatic action with numbering inconsistent with `rtp-autonomy-spectrum`. They are retired as permission rules. Use the current readiness assessment and explicit action rights, and preserve version context when quoting an older document.

## What would challenge the segmentation hypothesis?

Useful counter-tests include a shared design performing as well as tailored defaults on the relevant outcomes; direct task preferences predicting needs better than attitude labels; apparent segment differences disappearing after accounting for task or access; or tailored onboarding improving enthusiasm while leaving useful work unchanged.

Measure adverse effects and operating cost as well as the intended benefit. If treatment changes attitude, report the change separately from baseline-segment comparisons. A shift toward appropriate caution can be a successful result, and a willing niche can have real product fit without proving fit for everyone.
