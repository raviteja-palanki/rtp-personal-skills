# Authority and participation: a practical review

These are the original skill's twelve mechanism labels, retained as a diagnostic catalog. They combine practitioner examples and the library's deductions. They are not twelve established laws or an exhaustive threat model. Evidence may exist for every mechanism; absence from an org chart does not make a change intrinsically undetectable.

| # | Original mechanism | What to examine | Necessary qualification |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | From below, by prompt | Could an instruction or tool change suppress required escalation? Inspect change authority, enforcement, and test cases. | Prompt influence in a case does not prove that all governance is prompt-controlled. Enforce important boundaries outside discretionary model behavior where needed. |
| 2 | From above, by agility | Who can amend or retire a control, under which review, and with what replacement? | A quickly changeable structure can retain protected decision rights. Agility does not imply capture. |
| 3 | From the side, by tooling | Does a platform improve evidence and access, or obscure the need for expert challenge? | Easier reviews can strengthen governance. Reviewer standing is not solely a function of scarce expertise. |
| 4 | From within, by encoding the criterion | Who owns encoded standards, exceptions, and updates? Can affected cases challenge them? | Code, models, datasets, change logs, and reviews can leave evidence. Encoding does not necessarily end deliberation. |
| 5 | From above, by altitude—designed | Does an architectural owner retain the appropriate system-level controls and delegates for individual cases? | Governance at a higher level can be legitimate and effective. Not every owner needs personal override of every output. |
| 6 | Legitimate termination by protocol | Was dissent heard and recorded where relevant, and was the authorized decision made with clear responsibilities and reopening conditions? | Disagree-and-commit is not inherently a failure. Recording an objection alone does not make an unlawful or unauthorized decision legitimate. |
| 7 | Holder exits the regime / common parent | Are conflicts, recusals, delegations, and dispute resolution explicit? | Recusal can protect governance; a common executive can arbitrate without eliminating meaningful challenge. |
| 8 | Jurisdictional exit | Which activity and actor fall within which controls or obligations? Are external users and suppliers covered appropriately? | Legal boundaries and liability arrangements need an actual assessment. Activity outside an internal review can still leave contracts, transactions, and audit evidence. |
| 9 | Upward, by promotion | When a person changes roles, what authority and responsibility transfer, and to whom? | Promotion need not erode oversight. Inspect actual rights and practical access rather than title alone. |
| 10 | Visible self-exemption | Does a leader disregard rules, and how is the exception justified, challenged, or corrected? | Visibility can affect credibility; a single event does not prove every subordinate abandons the rule. Legitimate exceptions should be distinguishable from arbitrary privilege. |
| 11 | From above, by structural agility | Are review-time and adoption metrics balanced with evidence of control effectiveness and residual risk? | The predicted collapse “within two quarters” is unsupported. Hard-to-measure risk can still be evaluated through indicators, scenarios, tests, and judgment. |
| 12 | From the platform, by invisibility | Which embedded controls prevent, detect, or contain harm; who changes them; and what happens when they fail? | Logging is mainly evidentiary, while filtering or authorization may prevent action. Embedded controls can work without a person manually stopping each case. |

Mechanisms 2 and 11 preserve distinct concerns—who can change governance, and which measures shape that change. Mechanisms 5 and 9 distinguish deliberate organization design from role transitions. Mechanisms 7 and 10 distinguish a role's exemption from the consequences of visible conduct. Treat overlap as useful perspective, not independent evidence.

Recent Novel Insights also raises **deletion of an accountable role** during reorganization. Check the transfer of responsibilities, competence, relationships, and control access before a role disappears. This adds a relevant staffing check without claiming such changes can never be piloted, reversed, or evaluated.

## Authority evidence

Review the three structural questions—who can stop, whether they know, whether they have standing—and the fourth question about the cost of acting. Combine this with real case evidence, representative exercises, and the actual enforcement path. Separate “specified,” “implemented,” “exercised,” and “observed in operation.” These states are more informative than calling all untested authority fake.

The original stop-count protocol uses a twelve-month spot check or eight quarters of history. Neither window is a scientific threshold. A count of three problematic cases and zero stops is not automatically failure: a remedy short of stopping may have been appropriate. Review whether the material issue was resolved and who made that decision. Do not reward stopping for its own sake or require risky live exposure to prove authority.

The “steering wheel versus brakes” framing can prompt a useful distinction: shaping work early and intervening when necessary are compatible. A published statement that review should not impede progress does not prove a particular organization lacks veto authority. Ask what its process actually permits and has achieved.

## Participation and meaningful commitment

The original RACI/charter anecdotes describe documents that did not change work. Keep the question of receiving-team participation, but use a stronger test than whether text changed. Can the team explain the decisions? Has it accepted feasible responsibilities? Is time or capacity available? What happened when a real trade-off appeared?

The ledger later challenges its own “modified artifact means negotiation” test: a signed, co-written charter can still release no people or resources. Conversely, a standard policy can be adopted unchanged with full understanding. Use evidence appropriate to the commitment, including accepted responsibilities and actual work, without demanding visible sacrifice for every agreement.

The MedTech **5/20/5** discussion pattern—five minutes framing, twenty deliberating, five deciding—is a practitioner format to try, not a validated unique method. Clarify who decides, protect useful dissent, record the rationale, and adapt the time. Deliberation, independent review, or a written exchange can also be effective.
