# Opportunity Solution Tree: evidence and calculations

Reviewed 13 September 2026.

## What comes from Torres and what this library adds

[Teresa Torres’s primary OST guide](https://www.producttalk.org/2016/08/opportunity-solution-tree/) describes outcome, opportunities, solutions, and assumption tests. It advises choosing a target opportunity before generating solutions across the entire tree and assessing effort while exploring solutions. These distinctions correct the prior version’s early effort ranking of opportunities and blanket requirement for every branch to contain multiple developed solutions.

The [Product Talk glossary](https://www.producttalk.org/glossary-discovery-opportunity-solution-tree/) identifies opportunities as customer needs, pain points, and desires. They are not limited to complaints. The technology/evaluation labels in this skill are a local addition, not a missing feature that makes all ordinary OSTs unfit for AI planning. Feasibility and risk have a place in the original method’s assumption testing.

## Correct the scoring direction

The old instruction said “don’t multiply” and then prescribed sorting by impact × confidence. It also gave easier work a higher effort score but sorted ascending to prefer easier work. This revision makes the optional impact-confidence product explicit and uses an ordinary effort direction: lower means less effort. Scores organize judgment; multiplication of ordinal ratings does not create a measured expected return.

Assessing feasibility and effort at the solution/component level also resolves the worked example’s contradiction: the same alert-fatigue opportunity had both deterministic and AI approaches, despite the rule requiring exactly one opportunity label.

## Numbers are examples, not validation rules

The previous version used three-to-seven opportunities, two-to-four solutions, three-to-five bets, experiments below 10% of build cost, a thirty-minute logic test, and 30–50% deterministic roadmap work. These can describe particular plans but are not universal quality checks. Neither a minimum of two rejections nor an empty rejection list establishes good judgment.

The predictive-maintenance figures—30% downtime reduction, four quarters, 78% ignored alerts, 4.2-hour diagnosis, five asset classes, 70% acceptance, and fewer than 5% confident errors—are hypothetical. Especially for Tier-1 assets, the last figure must not be treated as an acceptable risk threshold. A complaint, acceptance, expert-agreement, and actual failure rate require different denominators.

## Novel Insights and prototype evidence

The ledger’s buildathon discussion around the assessment and prototype entries identifies two useful questions: whether the test’s rubric merely rewards what the conclusion claims is valuable, and whether a prototype clarifies the problem rather than only displaying execution. Its progress-bar observation suggests giving framing work a visible completion criterion. These are useful design hypotheses, not proof that production always crowds out reasoning or that more disagreements mean better discovery.

Apply the reader guide’s claim/test discipline: use an outcome measure outside the contest rubric before claiming general competitive advantage. A prototype that resolves a material uncertainty can succeed without creating a new question; one that creates many irrelevant questions can fail. Match the artifact to the decision.
