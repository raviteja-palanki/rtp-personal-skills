# Competitive map concept guide

Competitive analysis asks which alternatives can meet the customer's need and what sustains their value. AI makes model access, data rights, inference costs, and safety behavior more visible parts of that question. Product execution, usable features, distribution, and relationships still matter.

## Five patterns to investigate

**Surface parity can hide operating differences.** Two products may both offer search yet differ in relevant quality, freshness, access control, cost, and recovery. Compare the full task; a list of identical features is not evidence of equal value.

**Convergence is a scenario, not a clock.** A model upgrade may erode a narrow technical advantage, but the effect depends on the task and product integration. Proprietary training is neither automatically a moat nor automatically obsolete in six months. Current named-model comparisons need current tests and exact versions.

**Prices conceal costs.** A lower subscription price does not reveal margin or funding runway. Estimate plausible cost ranges and show what evidence would change them.

**Trust claims need scope.** A logo, “HIPAA-ready” phrase, or planned authorization is not equivalent to a valid scoped control report, compliant operation, or approval. Trust may affect access and willingness to pay, but does not establish a universal premium.

**Different moat mechanisms behave differently.** Learning from data requires valid signals and rights; context engineering can include copyable techniques and accumulated hard-to-reproduce assets; workflow integration can create value and switching effort. Examine the mechanism, complementary assets, replication cost, and substitutes. Repeated usage alone does not prove a self-improving flywheel.

The dimensions overlap. Safety, privacy, procurement evidence, and brand trust are not orthogonal measurements that can simply be added into a defensibility score.

## Four illustrative cases

These scenarios replace unsupported “real-world” claims in earlier versions. They do not establish historical company outcomes.

### 1 A model upgrade changes the comparison

A specialized assistant benefits from fine-tuning, workflow integration, and a tailored interface. A new general model matches some of its task performance. Re-evaluate which advantage narrowed, the customer's switching effort, and what new value the assistant can deliver. Do not infer that all such startups failed, that base-model accuracy substitutes for UX, or that model and workflow advantages have fixed six- and twenty-four-month lifetimes.

### 2 Lower costs expand choices

Suppose A charges $99/month and incurs $40 in the defined monthly cost unit; B charges $79 and incurs $20. Each has **$59 of contribution before omitted costs**. B has a higher percentage margin on this measure—about 74.7% versus 59.6%—but these numbers alone do not prove greater total profit, lower acquisition cost, more runway, or an ability to undercut forever.

Another scenario compares daily costs of $0.018, $0.05, and $0.095 for equivalent scope. At the same daily price of $0.15, contributions before omitted costs are $0.132, $0.10, and $0.055. The cheapest operator can also choose a premium price if customers value its offering. The expensive operator must justify its cost or change it; “regulated market” is not an automatic destination.

A support rival charging $800 rather than $2,000/month may appeal to price-sensitive buyers, but quality, requirements, service, distribution, and willingness to pay still need evidence. Enterprise controls add costs for any provider; low current inference cost does not prevent a firm from investing in them. Price-sensitive means more responsive to price, correcting the earlier reversed phrasing.

### 3 Data policy changes affect market access

Suppose a new customer contract limits training use or retention of its data. A provider that relied on that use must determine whether it can meet the new requirement and still deliver value. A provider already supporting the restriction may gain an advantage.

This is a contractual scenario, not a claim that a new law banned all PHI training. Applicable legal duties, authorization, agreements, de-identification, purpose, and setting require proper analysis. More retained data does not automatically yield better quality, and a deletion promise does not establish complete compliance.

### 4 Trust and price differ across segments

Consider this fictional comparison:

| Measure | A | B |
|---|---:|---:|
| Individual price/month | $40 | $25 |
| Enterprise price/month | $400 | $250 |
| Individual NRR | 75% | 85% |
| Enterprise NRR | 90% | 45% |

A's price is 1.6 times B's, a **60% premium**, not 30–50%. The table does not establish that trust caused the gap or that either company is profitable. NRR below 100% indicates net contraction in the measured existing-customer revenue cohort over the defined period; it does not measure total company growth. Check scope, cohorts, pricing units, acquisition, quality, service, and costs before interpreting the difference.

## Assumptions and limits

The analysis needs a reasonably defined decision, not a fully mature market. A pre-PMF team can compare existing ways of doing the job. Stealth rivals can be represented by scenarios and explicit unknowns rather than invented internals.

Cost advantage can matter greatly, but demand, constraints, and rival responses decide its strategic value. Market power, buyer or supplier leverage, substitutes, and entry barriers need case-specific analysis; a framework does not predict inevitable consolidation or margin compression by itself.

Porter's work on competition and Christensen's work on innovation provide broader conceptual context. They do not establish a universal shift from features to model efficiency. Earlier attributed essays and vendor positioning claims did not substantiate the fixed moat lifetimes or premiums.
