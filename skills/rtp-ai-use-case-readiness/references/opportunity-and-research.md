## Qualify an early opportunity: six behaviors

For a founder still establishing demand, use the Speed–Problem–Results–Implementation–Niche–Trust frame as a conversation aid:

| Behavior | Intended contribution | Question |
|---|---|---|
| Speed | Attention through prompt recognition of the situation | Can you describe this buyer's situation clearly and promptly? |
| Problem | Urgency | What changed, and why does solving the problem matter now? |
| Results | Belief in a useful outcome | Can the buyer explain the expected result to others without you? |
| Implementation | Confidence about adoption and risk | Have you addressed the practical concerns that could block use? |
| Niche | Repeatability | Does a defined buyer type, problem, and buying motion recur? |
| Trust | Permission to proceed | Can credibility transfer beyond the founder to evidence, delivery, and the team? |

Start with a sufficiently important problem. The source's manufacturer case reframed a generic pitch around revenue at risk, changing which opportunities looked promising. That is a reported case, not proof that the wording caused the pipeline outcome. Buyer silence may reflect implementation concerns, but also budget, priority, procurement, competition, or changed circumstances. Ask rather than diagnosing disbelief or hidden risk from silence alone.

Source in the local collection: Rubinstein and Onyemah, HBR, 24 June 2026; founder interviews and a German manufacturer case, with no effect sizes supplied. Use `rtp-fit-signal` for demand evidence. Early-stage status does not reduce the controls required for the proposed actions.

## Organizational readiness as a possible strategic asset

The six questions attributed to Westerman in “6 questions to guide your AI strategy,” MIT SMR, 3 August 2026, concern:

1. **Shared ambition:** how work should change and for whose benefit.
2. **Governance:** whether review informs design early and can restrict or stop an unsupported action.
3. **Scaling beyond a pilot:** how appropriate leaders stay engaged with decisions and evidence.
4. **Technical foundation:** the data, tools, and integration required for the chosen use, without trying to repair every organizational data issue first.
5. **Culture:** the ability to experiment, learn from evidence, and choose speed or caution according to consequence.
6. **Skills and reassurance:** what roles will change, what support is available, and what remains uncertain. Avoid promises the organization cannot keep.

The local article notes report examples rather than measured adoption effects. The **moat interpretation belongs to this library**, not to a validated conclusion of the source. Strong readiness can reveal accumulated operating knowledge, trusted relationships, or change capacity. To call that an advantage, test whether it produces value competitors cannot readily match, and what it costs to maintain. Some components can be purchased, hired, or imitated; model access is also not necessarily identical across firms.

Use readiness as a deployment condition and, when justified, as an input to `rtp-moat-finder` and `rtp-ai-portfolio-management`. Neither a trivial successful rollout nor a high self-assessed score proves a durable moat. The ledger's A/N framing is useful for asking who retains reusable value; it does not establish inevitable value capture or a fixed time for rivals to catch up.

## Which function might go first?

The procurement discussion suggests three questions:

- **Economic visibility:** can the function connect work to a meaningful outcome? Financial measures help where relevant; important strategic, service, or risk outcomes may need other measures.
- **Process structure at scale:** is there repeated work with enough shared structure to design and evaluate a system?
- **Judgment-heavy friction:** which specific points resist current automation, and why?

Failure of traditional automation is a clue, not proof that an agent fits. Poor incentives, missing data, integration cost, or an uneconomic task can explain the same history. Conversely, combining established automation with a limited AI component may be enough.

The local HBR article “Why Agentic AI Could Transform Procurement,” by Himmelreich, Oshri, Scala, and Zaidani, August 2026, reports a survey of 385 organizations: software development 35%, IT operations 31%, marketing 26%, and procurement 9%. The provided account does not disclose the sampling frame or adoption definition. Both the percentages **and their ordering** are descriptive of that reported sample; neither establishes population rankings, readiness, or which function should win economically. Procurement's lower adoption does not isolate organizational causes from technological or other causes.

Check accountability and approvals, operational ownership, and usable data before piloting. Approval can reduce throughput while still being necessary; test whether scope or control design can preserve value without removing a needed safeguard. Include the people affected by the tradeoffs alongside the accountable decision owner and required specialists.

The article's “belief stage” names activity celebrated without clear accountability for realized value. Counter it with a proportionate investment or learning case: process step, intended benefit, evidence, cost, owner, and next funding decision. An exploratory pilot can legitimately test whether a value lever exists; it should state that uncertainty rather than invent a financial KPI.

## Can the intended user judge and improve the output?

The local notes for Vendraminelli and colleagues' “Gen AI Won't Make Your Employees Experts” describe a controlled study of 78 employees at one UK fintech, using topic generation and article writing. The groups were daily writers, adjacent marketing specialists, and developers/data scientists. The primary page confirms the **March–April 2026** issue; the detailed figures below remain reported from the supplied notes, not independently reverified against a full study in this pass. [Primary article](https://hbr.org/2026/03/gen-ai-wont-make-your-employees-experts).

Reported results on a five-point rating scale:

- For topics, the difference between the highest- and lowest-scoring groups narrowed from 0.80 to 0.13. The notes report AI-assisted marketers slightly ahead of assisted writers and all assisted groups above unassisted writers.
- For writing with AI, reported averages were writers 3.96, marketers 3.92, and technologists 3.38; the technologists' unassisted mean was 3.42.

These point estimates do not establish statistical equivalence, a general zero effect for technologists, or an immutable boundary around domain vocabulary. The notes describe comparable model suggestions but differences in refinement; that supports investigating users' ability to evaluate and improve outputs. It does not show that an AI system can never help develop that ability.

Test the intended users on representative work: can they state the standard, detect a consequential flaw, distinguish a correct answer from a plausible error, improve or appropriately escalate it, and retain that performance under realistic workload? Topic selection may demand different knowledge from writing the finished article. Neither task proves that one must be a producer to evaluate all work in that domain.

Paste-through may flag missed review, but it is not the only signal of failure. An unchanged output may be correct; an edited output may remain wrong. Measure the quality of final work and interventions. Route capability formation and monitoring to `rtp-judgment-guard` instead of turning job titles or edit counts into release gates.

## Claims about new tasks and worker benefit

Acemoglu, Autor, and Johnson's 2026 *Building Pro-Worker Artificial Intelligence* distinguishes labor-augmenting, capital-augmenting, automating, expertise-leveling, and new task-creating change. The primary abstract supports this conceptual taxonomy. [NBER paper](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34854).

Compare work before and after the proposed deployment at a consistent, useful level of detail. An unchanged detailed task set weakens a claim of new-task creation in that scope; an unchanged job title or broad task label may hide new subtasks. New tasks can also appear elsewhere or later. Conceptual categories can inform an early hypothesis; establishing realized employment, wage, or expertise effects needs further evidence. Do not claim the framework is unusable until the entire labor market reprices the work.

A product can benefit workers without creating new tasks. Neither claim settles the permitted autonomy of its actions.

## Dependency and pilot lessons from Novel Insights

The HBR AI/IT conflict article in the local July 2026 collection uses anonymized advisory cases. Its foundation categories guide dependency mapping; they are not an exhaustive technical classification or measured probability of failure.

The ledger's pilot-capacity example contrasts participation across two sites. It supports asking whether test owners have time, incentives, and resources. It does not prove that every pilot must appear in a formal performance review or that all other causal variables were held constant.

The later roadmap comparison usefully separates organizational scope from consequence tolerance. A back-office action can be highly consequential; a customer-facing interaction can be tightly bounded. Order use cases by evidence, dependencies, and consequences rather than assuming a fixed march from personal to departmental to enterprise autonomy. Keep task-level productivity findings within their measured population when building a broader investment case.
