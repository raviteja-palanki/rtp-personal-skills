# Vision Setting

Describe the future experience the product aims to make possible and why that future is worth pursuing. A vision gives direction; strategy chooses where to compete and how to progress; the roadmap sequences learning and delivery. These inform one another. A clear destination helps choices but does not make every trade-off obvious.

For AI products, separate the enduring value sought from assumptions about a particular model, technique, or price. The value may be a human experience, a technical capability, or a broader organizational or public benefit. A developer platform has users and meaningful outcomes too; it does not need a sentimental story to have a vision.

## Set the scope and decision first

Identify the product or initiative, purpose, beneficiaries, audience, horizon, decision owner, and current problem with direction. Use existing customer research, strategy, and commitments. Ask only for missing information that would change the work.

Clarify what is open to change and what is fixed. Bring the people responsible for execution into the discussion, along with relevant user perspectives. Agreement on every detail is not required before drafting. A vision exercise can reveal differences that `purpose-dialogue` helps address.

Choose proportionate depth:

- **Full exploration** when a new initiative, major pivot, or changed ambition requires substantial customer, feasibility, and stakeholder work.
- **Focused refresh** when the direction remains useful but the language, assumptions, scope, or audience needs attention.
- **A small provisional statement** before product-market fit or during a crisis, if it helps choose the next experiment or preserve direction under constraints.

The old two-to-three-week exercise, one-week refresh, eighteen-month age trigger, $10 million ARR/100,000 DAU milestones, and six-month runway cutoff are not requirements. Do not delay urgent work for a large vision exercise. Do not assume priority conflict always means a missing vision: resource constraints, incentives, evidence, or decision rights may be the cause.

## 1. Establish the three inputs

### Who benefits?

Name a recognizable segment and its circumstances. Distinguish users, customers, buyers, and people affected by the product when they differ. “Knowledge workers” may be suitable for a broad initiative but usually needs detail for a product decision.

Use real customer evidence where available. Personas or composites can make the draft tangible, but label them and do not treat three invented customers as validation. A new product can begin with a hypothesized segment and a plan to test it.

### What changes?

Describe the before-and-after experience: what becomes possible, easier, safer, more reliable, or more worthwhile? Include what people continue to decide and where AI or other technology contributes.

Connect felt experience to observable evidence. Metrics can clarify the intended change; they do not have to be deferred until after the vision. Avoid promising a four-person workload without burnout, detection of every risk, or universal five-minute diagnosis unless those are explicitly aspirations rather than established capabilities.

When describing time or capacity released, say who receives the benefit and what enables it to become useful. Finishing earlier, reducing overload, improving quality, doing more valuable work, and increasing throughput can all be valid outcomes. Do not assume all released time must become additional work.

### Over what horizon?

Choose a horizon that fits the ambition and decision. A one-year view may support the next planning cycle, a three-year view may express a broader product direction, and a ten-year view may articulate a long-term purpose. These are examples, not a mandatory ladder.

Record what is known, assumed, or deliberately aspirational. Do not equate one year with one or two model generations or three years with three or four generations and a five-to-tenfold cost reduction. Assess relevant scenarios with `signal-scanner` and `capability-tracking`; scanning improves the evidence base without turning a forecast into certainty.

## 2. Draft a connected set of expressions

Develop three forms when a full vision package is useful. They should express the same destination at different levels of detail, not compete as three unrelated strategic choices. For a small request, deliver the needed form and retain the others only if they improve it.

### Narrative: the future experience

Use roughly three to five sentences describing a representative situation, the change, and why it matters. Make the product's intended behavior concrete. Label fictional people, simulated capabilities, and assumed performance.

**Illustrative narrative:** At the start of a shift, Rajesh reviews evidence of a developing compressor problem. The system explains what it observed, what remains uncertain, and which checks would help. He combines that information with his experience to decide whether to schedule maintenance. The team can prepare for a repair rather than discover the problem only after an avoidable shutdown.

A storyboard, mock-up, or vision prototype can convey the experience when prose is insufficient. A working prototype is not proof of production reliability or future performance. See `strategy-canvas` for how this connects to the broader planning process.

### Headline: the memorable direction

Aim for a short sentence, often fifteen words or fewer, without sacrificing meaning or making an absolute promise.

**Illustrative headline:** “Plant operators anticipate problems and make confident maintenance decisions with evidence they can trust.”

Brevity is a communication aid, not a test that proves strategic quality. A meaningful technical phrase may be appropriate for an expert audience; explain it for other readers.

### Team version: purpose, audience, and evidence of progress

Write three short parts:

- **Why this matters:** the consequential problem and why the organization should address it now.
- **Who it serves:** the intended user or beneficiary and the important context.
- **What progress looks like:** a few observable changes, including outcome measures and relevant experience signals.

For the industrial illustration, the user could be a shift lead in a selected plant type. Progress might include better maintenance decisions, fewer avoidable interruptions, manageable alert burden, and confidence calibrated to system reliability. Define the measurement before claiming improvement. A compelling narrative does not justify declaring the largest installed base, exclusive ability to solve the problem, or a proven 50% downtime reduction.

## 3. Review the five qualities

This is the library's practical synthesis, influenced by product-thinking work associated with Cagan, Doshi, and Bavaro. It is not a validated scoring instrument or a single framework quoted from those authors.

| Quality | Review question | What to clarify if weak |
|---|---|---|
| **Aspirational** | Is the intended future meaningful and worth pursuing? | State the change or continuing value that matters; avoid adding ambition merely to sound bold. |
| **Specific** | Can the reader understand who benefits and how? | Define scope and context sufficiently for the decision. A broad company vision may need narrower product interpretations. |
| **Durable** | Does the destination remain valuable under plausible changes? | Separate the desired value from volatile assumptions and identify what could invalidate it. |
| **Differentiating** | Does it express the intended contribution or focus? | Explain the relevant emphasis. A vision need not uniquely identify a company without its logo or itself establish a moat. |
| **Motivating** | Can the intended audience see a reason to care? | Test understanding, credibility, and relevance with people; do not assume every engineer or customer shares one emotional response. |

Use **supported**, **needs work**, or **not yet tested**, with reasons. Do not manufacture five passes by changing the wording alone. A weak area may be a research gap rather than an editing problem. Retain the question it raises and the next step.

### Test durability without inventing a guarantee

Ask whether the benefit still matters if capability improves substantially, prices change, or alternatives become common. The old tenfold-better and tenfold-cost scenarios can be useful stress cases when made concrete; define what capability improves and which cost increases. They are not universal acceptance tests.

A vision can remain meaningful while becoming temporarily uneconomic, requiring a different delivery path or being deferred. Conversely, attractive wording does not prove that value will exceed a tenfold cost increase. A technological breakthrough that fully achieves the desired state can make a vision accomplished rather than invalid.

A large context window does not eliminate the need for summarization. Naming AI, a model, or a technique is not automatically wrong when it defines the product's purpose or constraints. Keep implementation details in the appropriate layer and make their role clear.

## 4. Check the four recurring wording failures

| Pattern | Signal to investigate | Revision move |
|---|---|---|
| **Capability as the whole vision** | A model or technique is named without the value it enables | Explain the useful outcome and separate delivery assumptions. |
| **Feature catalog** | The statement lists functions but leaves their combined benefit unclear | Ask what users could accomplish if those features worked well. Keep the feature list as supporting detail. |
| **Industry jargon** | Readers cannot explain the intended change in concrete terms | Use familiar language for the audience and explain necessary specialist terms. |
| **Borrowed competitor identity** | “The X for Y” is doing all the explanatory work | State the domain need, proposed contribution, and limits of the analogy. |

These are diagnostic patterns, not syntax bans. Three uses of “and” do not prove a feature-list failure, and an analogy can be useful shorthand. Similar visions can be valid for competitors; strategy must explain their distinct choices and economics.

## 5. Connect the vision to actual commitments

Review the current Now/Next/Later work and relevant resource allocation with the people who own it. For each material item, identify its contribution or tension:

- **Direct progress:** contributes to the intended outcome.
- **Necessary support or obligation:** enables reliable delivery, fulfills a commitment, or meets an essential requirement. Maintenance, accessibility, safety, and compliance are not automatically expendable distractions.
- **Learning:** tests the vision, user need, or a critical assumption.
- **Potential mismatch:** has weak relevance or conflicts with the stated direction; investigate before deciding.

Name the priorities the vision strengthens, exclusions it supports, and unresolved trade-offs. Do not require three priorities, three cuts, or removal of 30% of the roadmap. An already coherent roadmap can remain unchanged. A clearer decision rule or a needed validation study can be the most useful immediate result.

If calculating the share of investment serving the vision, state the denominator—people, money, time, or weighted commitments. Counting bets equally can hide their different sizes. The old 60%, 75%, and 40–50% allocation thresholds were not empirically calibrated gates. Assess capacity, dependencies, and obligations rather than passing a fixed percentage.

Investigate apparent contradictions. A product that promises useful explanations does not necessarily require an interpretable underlying model, and explanation alone does not establish trust or correctness. Determine what users can verify and which controls they need. An executive dashboard can support the operator outcome even when operators are the primary users. Resolve conflict through scope, sequence, design, resources, or a change to the vision as appropriate.

Conclude with one of three descriptive judgments:

- **Coherent:** major commitments and dependencies support the direction.
- **Drifting:** some commitments or assumptions need reconciliation.
- **Disconnected:** the stated direction and actual operating choices materially diverge.

Explain the evidence and decision needed; these are not scores with fixed cutoffs. Participation can improve understanding, but edits, disagreement, and roadmap cuts are not proof of commitment. Use `purpose-dialogue` when the underlying issue is purpose, influence, or implementation ownership.

## 6. Check what the vision measures and enables

Volume and speed can be valuable when they remove a real constraint. Pair them with quality, downstream value, and relevant costs. Three times as many proposals, four times as many accounts covered, or a 60% shorter cycle are incomplete claims without the outcome and guardrails; they are not inherently worthless metrics.

If the intended benefit is richer client relationships, explain how time becomes available, who controls its use, and what good work and outcomes would look like. Retention may help assess the result, but it has other causes and may lag. If the aim is quicker emergency response or less waiting, cycle time may itself be closely tied to the benefit.

Avoid a false ceiling on efficiency. Costs have a lower bound, but a cost saving can create a large percentage increase in profit when starting margins are small. Growth also requires resources and can destroy value. The earlier roughly 10% efficiency-value ceiling was an illustrative argument, not a general financial law. Use `cost-model` and `ai-portfolio-management` for the actual comparison.

Consider the conditions for useful judgment, including information, authority, time, workload, and opportunity to learn. Do not promise that a vision statement or a renamed time allocation supplies those conditions.

## 7. Adapt the communication and name the next decision

For a full package, use the same core vision with detail suited to the audience:

- **Board or sponsor:** the problem, ambition, strategic relevance, evidence, and important uncertainties.
- **Engineering and delivery teams:** the intended experience, boundaries, dependencies, and what is stable versus open to change.
- **Customers or users:** the benefit, credible experience, responsibilities, and limits relevant to them.

These are starting points, not exclusive interests. All audiences may need costs, risks, or technical detail. Use `stakeholder-communications` for the fuller adaptation.

Name the next concrete action by role and date. It may be a product decision, a research session, a prototype, an allocation discussion, or confirmation that the current priorities remain appropriate. Avoid treating publication of the vision as evidence of adoption.

## Deliverable

```text
VISION — [product, owner, date]
Purpose and scope: [why this work matters; what is open or fixed]
Beneficiaries: [segment, context, evidence or hypothesis]
Transformation: [before/after; human and technical responsibilities]
Horizon: [timeframe and rationale; key capability/economic assumptions]
Narrative: [future experience; illustrative claims labeled]
Headline: [short expression of the same direction]
Team version: [why, who, observable progress]
Five-quality review: [supported / needs work / not tested, with reasons]
Durability scenarios: [what remains valuable; what could change the path]
Priorities and boundaries: [support, learning, exclusions, open trade-offs]
Coherence: [coherent / drifting / disconnected; evidence and resolution]
Audience adaptation: [as needed]
Next decision: [action, owner, date, evidence needed]
```

Before concluding, verify that the forms agree, the user and benefit are understandable, numerical promises and scenarios have the right status, and priorities reflect actual constraints. Check all four wording patterns and the most consequential assumptions. An aspiration is not a performance guarantee or a release criterion.

Use `first-principles` for deeper assumptions, `signal-scanner` for changes that could affect the direction, and `strategy-canvas` to connect the vision to objectives, alternatives, allocations, and conditional bets. These processes may iterate; do not enforce a rigid sequence that requires a locked vision before useful strategy work can begin.

Follow the [Universal Skill Protocol](../../../UNIVERSAL-SKILL-PROTOCOL.md) for a proportionate trade-off ledger and handoff. Close with the recommended working vision, hypothesis and falsifier, main trade-off, biggest risk and mitigation, and next action. Use the requested output format.

When a visual would help, `excalidraw-svg` can show the future experience or the connection from vision to priorities and next decisions. Avoid decorative scores or a dense diagram that combines every framework. The [examples and evidence notes](references/examples-and-evidence.md) preserve the original industrial case and its qualifications.
