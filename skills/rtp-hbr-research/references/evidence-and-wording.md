# Evidence and wording guide

Reference revision 3.7.1 — reviewed 13 September 2026.

## Keep the claim attached to its evidence

The three existing glyphs support indexing and review. Use their text labels as well when needed; they summarize provenance, not the probability that a claim is true.

| Label | Use | Do not infer |
|---|---|---|
| `✅` Audited | A figure actually covered by an identified audit or equivalent verified record; specify the scope | Every statement in a filing, court docket, or peer-reviewed article is audited |
| `◆` Disclosed | A company's own statement or a study's reported finding, with method and limitations | Independent validation, replication, causality, or organization-wide applicability |
| `⚠` Reported or unresolved | A secondary report, forecast, unresolved claim, or analyst inference, described accurately | That every item with this label is equally weak or false |

Describe the evidence rather than letting the glyph do all the work. A primary company announcement establishes what the company disclosed. An underlying study can establish its methods and reported result. Peer review is relevant but does not turn a study into an audited company outcome. A legal complaint can contain allegations; identify the document and procedural status rather than treating a docket as proof of the allegations.

Capture the number, unit, denominator, population, time period, method, comparison, and relevant uncertainty. Distinguish percentage points from relative change, run-rate from booked revenue, capacity released from cash saved, availability from usage, adoption from value, and task performance from organization-wide outcomes.

Use primary records for consequential company claims when accessible. A major newspaper can be a useful report or an original interview; its reputation alone does not make it the underlying primary record. Two genuinely independent sources can strengthen corroboration, but repeating the same study through two articles does not. A well-documented primary study may be more useful than several derivative reports. Record the actual evidence basis rather than forcing a two-link quota for every number.

When a claim cannot be resolved, state the limitation or omit it from a factual conclusion. Removing its number or replacing it with “substantial improvement” does not repair missing evidence. Internal `[VERIFY]` entries should distinguish:

- **Not yet checked:** identify the probable source and next check.
- **Searched but unavailable or not found:** record what was searched and what remains unknown. Do not claim no document exists solely because a search failed.
- **Known pending or embargoed evidence:** identify the source, status, and expected release if known. A future monitoring action requires the user's requested scope; writing a watch note does not schedule one.

If a source has been confirmed not to report the required grain, record that specific limit. Do not generalize that a client-owned company files no relevant reports.

## Apply the historical near-miss checks carefully

1. **Completed periods versus forecasts:** an “in its first year” outcome needs a completed measurement period. Before that, distinguish projection, annualization, or a different historical period. The Barnes Group example in earlier notes is a reason to inspect dates, not proof that every first-year phrase is wrong.
2. **Provisioning versus use:** access promised to students or licenses made available do not establish active users.
3. **Measurable versus measured:** a speaker's claim that gains are measurable is not a supplied measurement. State what evidence is missing; do not soften it into a verified usability benefit.
4. **Denominator and scope:** task gains can differ from end-to-end gains. Earlier Vanguard coding and lifecycle percentages illustrate that distinction; they do not establish a rule to halve every task-level estimate.
5. **Counterfactual quality:** a modeled comparison can be useful if assumptions and uncertainty are explicit. It is not inherently worse than having no comparison, and it is not an observed baseline.
6. **Publication and method:** judge a working paper, field experiment, interview, or model by the actual design and limitations. A university masthead does not guarantee rigor, and an unrefereed paper is not automatically the weakest source.
7. **Correct company attribution:** an interview can quote a vendor's earlier trial at a different organization. Confirm who experienced the outcome.
8. **Source independence:** record shared studies and datasets behind multiple articles or ledger entries.
9. **Date provenance:** a browser print header is a capture date. Inspect the article or episode dateline and official metadata before assigning a publication date.

Disclose relevant interests precisely: an author's equity stake, a recommended free tool they built, a vendor's own study, or a consultant's client evidence are different situations. An interest is context for assessment, not automatic disqualification.

## Write plainly without drifting from the source

Ravi's preference is to retain an author's clear wording when useful. Preserve exact technical terms and use concise, attributed quotations within applicable rights and quotation limits. Ordinary factual explanation can be paraphrased clearly. Do not copy whole passages merely because the original is well written, or present a distinctive borrowed sentence as Ravi's own prose.

In Part 7, keep a `Plain lines` block with brief examples of the source's explanatory register. Two or three lines are a useful aim when permitted, not a quota overriding quotation limits. If more text cannot be reproduced, record a paraphrase and a source location.

Use four checks:

- **Length:** would a shorter sentence preserve the same mechanism, condition, and evidence? The source's sentence is a comparison point, not a maximum length.
- **Verb:** prefer clear verbs. Keep a source verb when accurate, but correct a loose article claim if the underlying record supports a different description.
- **Direction:** paraphrasing can become plainer or more ornate. Choose the clearest accurate wording; drift is possible, not inevitable or one-directional.
- **Read aloud:** does the explanation sound like something a thoughtful colleague could follow on first reading?

The understandability check outranks a mechanical word list. Ask whether the paragraph states a clear point, explains the mechanism, and uses each clause for necessary information. A model's silent reading is a desk check; claim a human comprehension test only when a person actually participated.

Watch the five recurring shapes from the historical retrofit: unnecessarily grand verbs, unsupported superlatives, empty “hidden insight” introductions, dramatic negative lists, and monotonous paragraph rhythm. Clear headings or a repeated fifteen-part schema can still be useful. Do not change protected syntax, source quotations, legal section symbols, article keys, or accurate technical terms to satisfy a stylistic preference.

Use the current thinking-and-writing skill for tone. Humanizer patterns help diagnose awkwardness; they do not detect authorship. Preserve a warranted point of view without inventing feelings, personal experience, or certainty. A conclusion can be useful without a mandatory emotional aside or new pattern.
